01TextJul 1 – Jul 16

Be someone

Click a country and a year and you’re dealt people who could have lived there. Pick one, and their day streams in as prose, a scene at a time, each ending on a choice.

It began as a choose-your-own-adventure engine with a command-line player. Within ten days it had a day loop with memory and a five-act shape, a shared scene cache so readers who make the same choice share a story, free-text actions, and five “day grammars” for different kinds of day.

Improvised pacing broke almost at once. One afternoon ran to 38 scenes; one farmer got seven names in a single day. So the day began to be dealt ahead of time: a planned set of people, places and questions that the writer then performs.

What broke

Reading about a day didn’t put you in it. Every scene already carried a painted still, and the stills were doing the work of making it a place.

02Visual novelJul 15 – Sep 23

Put them on a stage

Painted backgrounds for places, cel-animated actors for people, every line voiced, and a deck of choices written in the character’s own voice.

The art direction was Satoshi Kon’s Paprika: cel figures over painted plates. The cast moved into a database with generated expressions, the writer started directing the actors, and each character got a voice, with lip-sync following their emotional state. It shipped on the web on July 23 and on iOS in September.

This was the busiest stretch. Most of the design happened in writing: briefs, rules, and a design doc corrected as live replays turned things up. Once it was playable, it had to look like a visual novel, with models, backgrounds and dialogue boxes.

What broke

Action is contact between layers. A figure from one generated image can’t lean on a desk from another, because they come from two painters with no shared reference.

03FilmSep 23 – Oct 4

One painter

Make the whole stage a film. A writer plans each five-to-eight-second shot, and MiniMax H3 renders it starting from the last frame of the shot before, so the person and the desk come from the same painting.

On September 2, a survey of video models concluded that real-time video wasn’t ready for the story. Three weeks later H3 rendered a six-second shot faster than you could read it, and the plan changed within a day.

The life film played a whole day with a fork at each decision: two options and “something else,” on a timer. It opened as a closed alpha for streamers, because one render watched by a whole chat costs a fraction per viewer. By October 3 a film of Eeyore’s Birthday was the front door.

What broke

A personal render per reader doesn’t pay for itself. The picture wouldn’t hold either: the model drifted toward Disney’s bear within two clips. And a film sets its own pace, so the reader can only wait.

04ComicsOct 5 – now

Drawn as you read

Paint still pictures, letter them in code, and let the reader turn the page.

It started on October 5 with The Rent, a Nathan for You parody drawn live. A day later every life on sonder.gg read as a comic, eight pages in four acts. The pictures are painted with no words in them. The lettering is code, which finds the faces so balloons stay off them and tails point at whoever is speaking.

Before a panel is shown, two separate looks check it for a missing or extra character, someone off model or off style, stray marks, or the wrong action. A slip buys one retake, and you see the take with the fewest slips. Eeyore’s Birthday is Milne’s chapter six, written past the book; at each decision the deck offers two roads, a line of your own, or the end.

Since October 15 a comic can also start from a concept instead of a life. The writer plans the whole story first, then writes the next stretch while the current panels paint.

One painter

Each new form fixed the last one’s continuity: the stage over loose stills, film over the stage’s seams, a checked panel over a drifting film.

Process

How it was built

sonder is built by a small team directing a lot of coding agents: 3,516 commits and 756 merged pull requests since July 1, and 1,034 commits since comics began on October 5. My job is less drawing screens and more deciding what’s true about the product, catching what’s wrong in live replays, and writing it down precisely enough that the next hundred commits follow it.

That’s also why the form could change three times in four months. Undoing things was cheap. The expensive part was knowing what to undo.
